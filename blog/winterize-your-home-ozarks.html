Winterizing Your Home in the Ozarks: Prevention Costs Less Than an Emergency

By Brian Beard, Beard's Home Services · Mountain Home, AR

Here's something I've seen over and over in 20-plus years of fixing houses around Mountain Home: the winter calls that hurt are almost never surprises. A pipe on an outside wall that froze last year freezes again. A hose left on the spigot splits the line inside the wall. A lake house sits empty with the heat turned way down, and nobody finds the leak for a week. Every one of those starts as a ten-minute fix in the fall and ends as a weekend of tearing out wet drywall and flooring.

Ozarks winters are mostly mild, which is exactly why people get caught. We get a handful of hard cold snaps — single digits or teens for a couple of nights — and houses here weren't always built with that in mind. Prevention is cheap. Emergency calls, water damage and insurance deductibles aren't.

1. Outside Faucets and Hoses

  • Disconnect every garden hose. A hose left attached traps water in the faucet, and even a "frost-free" spigot can't drain with a hose on it. This one causes more split pipes than anything else I see.
  • Shut off and drain older spigots. If your outside faucet has a shutoff valve inside the house or crawlspace, close it, then open the outside faucet to let it drain.
  • Cover them. Foam faucet covers are cheap at any hardware store and worth it for the cold nights.

2. Pipes in Crawlspaces, Garages and Outside Walls

Pipes freeze where cold air reaches them — crawlspaces, unheated garages, under cabinets on outside walls, and anywhere a pipe runs near a vent or a gap in the siding.

  • Wrap exposed pipes in foam pipe insulation. For the worst spots, use heat tape made for pipes, installed by the directions.
  • Close up gaps where pipes and wires go through walls and the foundation.
  • On the coldest nights, open the cabinet doors under sinks on outside walls and let the faucet drip slightly. Moving water is much harder to freeze.

3. Know Where Your Main Water Shutoff Is

If a pipe does burst, every minute it runs means more damage. Find your main shutoff now — not with water spraying across the floor. Make sure it turns, and show everyone in the house where it is. If it's stuck or corroded, that's worth fixing before winter.

4. Well Houses and Pump Lines

Plenty of homes around Baxter County are on wells. A frozen pressure tank or pump line means no water at all until it thaws — or a cracked tank. Check that your well house is sealed up, the pipes are insulated, and whatever keeps it above freezing (a well-house heater or heat lamp) actually works and is safely mounted away from anything that can burn.

5. Lake Houses and Homes That Sit Empty

A lot of Twin Lakes homes sit empty part of the winter, and that's where the worst damage happens because nobody's there to catch it.

  • Don't turn the heat off. Keep the thermostat no lower than about 55°F.
  • If the house will be empty for a long stretch, shut off the main water and drain the lines, or have someone do it.
  • Have a neighbor or someone you trust check on it after every hard freeze.

6. Heat, Air and Safety

  • Change the furnace filter and have the system serviced before you need it every day.
  • Test smoke detectors and carbon monoxide detectors. Heating season is when CO problems show up — especially with gas furnaces, wood stoves and space heaters.
  • Keep space heaters away from curtains, bedding and anything that burns, and plug them straight into the wall, not an extension cord.

7. Seal Out the Drafts

Worn weatherstripping and gaps around doors and windows let cold air in and money out. New door sweeps, weatherstripping and a bead of caulk are some of the cheapest improvements you can make, and you'll feel the difference the first cold night.

8. Roof, Gutters and Deck

Water and ice do their damage from the outside too. Clogged gutters overflow and freeze, and loose roof flashing lets melting snow inside. I covered both of these in detail already — see fall gutter cleaning and drainage and getting your deck ready for winter.

The Short Version

Pull the hoses, wrap the exposed pipes, find your water shutoff, keep the heat on in empty houses, check the well house, and test your detectors. That's an afternoon of work and a few dollars in supplies. The alternative is a 2 a.m. phone call, a flooded crawlspace, and a repair that costs many times more than the prevention would have.
